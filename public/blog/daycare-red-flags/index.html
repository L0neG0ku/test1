12 daycare red flags parents should never ignore

12 daycare red flags to watch for on a visit and after enrolment, from locked-off rooms to vague pickup rules, and what to do if you spot one.

By The Kribble team · 17 October 2026 · 5 min read

  2. Red flags after enrolment
  3. What to do if you spot a red flag
  4. Use red flags as part of a bigger picture

The biggest daycare red flags are hidden rooms, staff who ignore or snap at children, and vague answers about safety, pickup or fees. After enrolment, watch for unexplained injuries, sudden changes in your child, and a centre that gets defensive when you ask questions.

One red flag doesn't always mean a bad centre. But each one deserves a clear answer before you trust them with your child.

Red flags on a visit

1. You can't see the rooms where children spend the day

A good centre shows you the classrooms, nap room, toilets and kitchen. It might ask you to keep quiet during naps, which is fair.

If you are kept in the office or reception, or told you can only visit by appointment outside working hours, ask why. Openness is a basic sign of a centre with nothing to hide.

2. Staff ignore crying children or speak harshly

Watch how caregivers respond when a child is upset. A warm centre comforts quickly and calmly.

Shouting, sarcasm, threats or leaving a child to cry alone for long periods are all reasons to walk away.

3. Too many children for the adults in the room

Count the adults and children yourself. If one adult is clearly stretched across a large group of toddlers, attention and safety suffer.

Our teacher-to-child ratio guide explains what is reasonable for each age.

4. Anyone can walk in

Notice how you got in. An unlocked gate, no sign-in and no one asking who you are suggests the same is true at pickup time.

5. Vague answers about who can collect a child

Ask what happens if someone other than you turns up at pickup. A good answer is specific: a written authorisation list, photo ID and a call to you.

"We know all the parents" is not a policy.

6. Dirty toilets, kitchen or nappy area

Young children put everything in their mouths. Look at the places that are hardest to keep clean: toilets, changing areas, the kitchen and the floor where babies crawl.

A strong smell, overflowing bins or no hand-washing after nappy changes are clear warning signs.

7. Obvious safety hazards

Look for uncovered sockets, unguarded stairs or balconies, open windows, loose wires, and cleaning products or medicines within reach. These are simple to fix. A centre that hasn't fixed them may not be paying attention.

Our daycare safety checklist has 20 things to check.

8. No staff checks

Ask whether every staff member, including helpers, drivers and cleaners, has been background or police verified. Hesitation, or "only the teachers", is a red flag.

9. Pressure to pay on the spot

Be cautious of "only one seat left, pay the admission fee today" or a large non-refundable deposit before you've had time to think. A good centre gives you fees in writing and time to decide.

Our guide to daycare fees explains what's normal and what to ask about refunds.

Red flags after enrolment

10. Incidents you hear about late, or not at all

Small bumps and scrapes happen with young children. What matters is that you're told the same day, with a short explanation.

If you find bruises or marks that no one mentions, or the explanation changes, ask for a meeting.

11. Defensive or evasive answers

You should be able to ask questions and get calm, direct replies. If staff become irritated, change the subject or tell you to "just trust us", take note.

The same goes for a centre that discourages you from dropping in or calling during the day.

12. A lasting change in your child

Some tears at drop-off are normal, especially in the first few weeks. Our guide to settling into daycare explains what usually happens.

Pay attention if your child becomes fearful of a particular person, refuses to go after settling well, or shows other changes that last. You know your child best. If something feels wrong, trust that feeling and look into it.

What to do if you spot a red flag

Before you enrol: ask about it directly. Sometimes there is a good explanation. If there isn't, or the answer doesn't reassure you, move on. There are other centres.

After you enrol:

  1. Write down what you saw or heard, with dates and times.
  2. Ask for a meeting with the centre head, not just a word at pickup.
  3. Ask for specifics: what happened, who was there and what has changed since.
  4. If the answer doesn't satisfy you, consider moving your child.
  5. If you believe your child has been harmed, contact the police and seek medical care for your child. Your child's safety comes before any fee or contract.

Talk to other parents at the centre if you feel comfortable. You may not be the only one who has noticed.

Use red flags as part of a bigger picture

Red flags are the "no" list. You also need a "yes" list: warm staff, a sensible routine, clean spaces and good communication. Our complete guide to choosing a daycare covers both, and our questions to ask on a visit help you test each area.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a red flag if a daycare doesn't allow surprise visits?

Not always. Some centres limit drop-ins during naps or settling-in times to avoid upsetting children. But a centre should welcome you at some point during a normal day, and should never keep you away entirely.

What should I do if my child comes home with an unexplained injury?

Ask the centre that day for an explanation and for their incident record. Young children do get small bumps, but staff should already have told you. If the answer is unclear or keeps changing, ask for a meeting with the centre head.

Is crying at drop-off a sign of a bad daycare?

Usually not. Many children cry at drop-off for the first few weeks and settle soon after you leave. Ask staff how long the crying lasts. A lasting change in your child, or fear of a specific person, deserves a closer look.

Should I leave a daycare after one bad experience?

It depends on how the centre responds. A centre that explains what happened, owns any mistake and tells you what it has changed may deserve another chance. One that gets defensive or vague is harder to trust.
