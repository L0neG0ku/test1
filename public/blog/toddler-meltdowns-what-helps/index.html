Why toddlers can't "just calm down", and what actually helps

Toddler meltdowns happen because self-control is still developing. Here is what helps in the moment, and what to ask your child's daycare about it.

Toddler meltdowns happen because a young child's ability to manage big feelings is still being built. They are not being naughty, and they cannot "just calm down" on command. What helps most is a calm adult nearby who lends them some of that calm until the storm passes.

Why a toddler can't switch the feeling off

Think of self-control as a skill, like walking or talking. Toddlers are still learning it.

The parts of the brain that help us pause, think and choose a response keep developing through early childhood and well beyond. A two-year-old feels frustration, hunger or disappointment at full volume. The brakes are not fully fitted yet.

When the banana breaks, your child isn't being dramatic. To them, it is a real loss they can't yet put in perspective.

Common triggers

Most meltdowns have a simple cause underneath. Look for these first:

  • Tiredness: a missed nap or a late night.
  • Hunger: a long gap between meals.
  • Transitions: leaving the park, switching off a toy, getting into the car seat.
  • Not being understood: they know what they want but can't say it.
  • Too much: noise, crowds, a busy day of new faces.
  • Big changes: a new sibling, a move, or starting daycare.

Spot a pattern and you can often prevent the next one.

Co-regulation: lending your calm

Children learn to calm themselves by being calmed by someone else, many times over. This is called co-regulation.

When you stay steady, your voice, face and breathing send a signal: "This is big, but we're okay." Over months and years, your child starts to do this for themselves.

It does not mean giving in. You can hold the limit ("We're not buying the toy") and stay kind at the same time.

Three steps that help in the moment

1. Get down to eye level

Kneel or sit so you are at their height. Standing over a crying child can feel like a threat, even when you mean well.

Keep your voice low and slow, and use few words.

2. Name the feeling

Say what you see, simply. "You're angry. You wanted to stay at the park."

Naming the feeling tells your child you understand. Over time it also gives them the words to say it instead of screaming it.

Avoid "Stop crying" or "It's nothing". To them, it is not nothing.

3. Wait

This is the hard part. Stay close and let the feeling run its course.

You don't have to fix it or talk them out of it. If they want a hug, offer one. If they push you away, stay nearby and say, "I'm right here."

Once they are calm, talk briefly about what happened or simply move on.

Meltdowns at daycare

Many parents worry when they hear about a meltdown at daycare. It is common, especially in the first weeks. If your child is new, our guide to settling into daycare covers what is normal.

What matters is how the staff respond. You want carers who stay calm, comfort the child and keep them safe, rather than shaming or isolating them.

What to ask a daycare

Add these to your list of questions to ask on a visit:

  • "What do you do when a child is very upset?" A good answer sounds like: "One of us stays with them, gets down to their level and helps them calm down."
  • "Do you use time-outs?" Listen for whether the child is left alone. Staying close is better at this age.
  • "How do you tell parents about a hard day?" Look for honest, specific updates, not just "fine".
  • "How do you handle a child who bites or hits during a meltdown?" You want a calm plan: separate, comfort both children, tell both families.

The way a centre handles big feelings tells you a lot about its care overall. It belongs on your daycare checklist alongside safety and hygiene.

It also helps to know what daily updates to expect from your daycare.

When to talk to someone

Most toddler meltdowns are a normal part of growing up. If you are worried about how often they happen, how long they last, or anything else about your child's development, speak to your paediatrician. They know your child and can guide you.

Frequently asked questions

Why do toddlers have so many meltdowns?

Toddlers feel strong emotions but are still developing the ability to manage them. Tiredness, hunger, transitions and not being understood are common triggers. Meltdowns usually become shorter and less frequent as language and self-control grow.

Should I ignore a toddler's tantrum?

Ignoring the feeling usually doesn't help a toddler calm down. You can hold a limit while staying close and calm. Getting to eye level, naming the feeling and waiting with them tends to work better than walking away.

Is it normal for my child to have meltdowns at daycare?

Yes, especially in the first few weeks or after a change at home. What matters is that staff respond calmly and comfort your child. Ask the centre how they handle big feelings and how they will let you know.

How can I prevent toddler meltdowns?

You can't prevent all of them, but you can reduce many. Keep naps and meals regular, warn your child before transitions, and keep busy days short. Spotting your child's own triggers helps most.
